If your applications keep disappearing into silence, it is not necessarily a question of ability. More often, the format or the content simply does not match what employers in Taiwan expect to see.

The Taiwanese Chinese-language resume follows its own set of conventions, and they differ considerably from the European or American format. Here are the 5 points that matter most, and how to bring your resume in line with them.

---

## 1. Include a photo

Almost every Taiwanese resume carries a photo. This surprises applicants from countries where photos are discouraged or even prohibited, but a resume without one looks incomplete here, and can suggest you have not researched local practice.

**What the photo should look like:**

- A formal headshot, ideally against a white or plain background
- Neat clothing, business or business-casual
- Sharp, recent, and taken for the purpose. Do not crop a selfie or a holiday snapshot

---

## 2. State your nationality and visa status clearly

When a Taiwanese employer opens the resume of a foreign applicant, the first question in their mind is almost always: can this person legally work in Taiwan?

Do not make them guess. Put it in the personal details section:

- Nationality
- Current residency status (student visa, post-graduation extension of stay, open work-based residency, and so on)
- Whether you need the employer to sponsor your work permit

Spelling this out shows your eligibility at a glance and saves both sides from wasted rounds of email.

---

## 3. Quantify your experience instead of listing duties

What a Taiwanese employer wants from your work history is the result you produced, not the tasks you were assigned.

**Before and after:**

❌ "Responsible for social media management"

✅ "Managed 3 social platforms, grew followers 40% in 3 months, and doubled the average engagement rate per post"

One principle covers most of it: **try to attach a number to every line of experience.** Headcount, budget, percentage, duration. Any measurable figure carries more weight than a vague description.

## 4. Be specific about languages, and attach test scores

For overseas Chinese and international students, language ability is a real advantage, but only if you describe it precisely. "Fluent Chinese" on its own says very little.

A format that works:

- Chinese: native / fluent (spoken and written) / comfortable in daily conversation
- English: TOEIC 800 / IELTS 6.5 / able to handle business correspondence
- Other languages: Malay (native), Indonesian (fluent)

If you hold a formal certification, include the score. It is the most direct evidence you have. Without one, clear level words such as "fluent" or "basic" still work.

---

## 5. Keep the personal statement to 300 to 500 characters

Many Taiwanese employers ask for a 自傳, a short personal statement. Applicants tend to err in one of two directions: two throwaway sentences, or a thousand words nobody will finish.

**Three things the statement has to answer:**

1. Who you are, and what relevant background you bring
2. Why Taiwan, and why this industry or role
3. Where you want your career to go, and what you can contribute to this company

Between 300 and 500 Chinese characters is the right length. Structure matters more than volume.

---

## One more thing: write it in traditional Chinese

This is easy to overlook. Even if you learned simplified characters, a resume sent to a Taiwanese company should be written in **traditional Chinese**. A simplified-character resume can read as a lack of seriousness about the Taiwanese market.

If you are unsure how to convert it, Microsoft Word and Google Docs both have a simplified-to-traditional conversion function. Run it, then check the output by hand.

---

## In short

The core principle of a Taiwanese resume is simple: **let the employer see your strongest points in the shortest possible time.** Photo, status information, quantified results, language ability, and a tight personal statement. Get those 5 right and your response rate will visibly improve.
