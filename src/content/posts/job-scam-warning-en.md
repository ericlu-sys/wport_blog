For overseas Chinese and international students looking for work in Taiwan, the risk is not only failing to find a job. Sometimes it is running into an outright scam. The people behind these schemes know that international students are still unfamiliar with the Taiwanese labor market, and they design the traps to look like ordinary job openings.

This article walks through the most common job scams here, and what you can do to identify them and protect yourself.

---

## Common job scam patterns

### Pattern 1: high pay, easy work, no experience needed

"Monthly salary NT$80,000 and up, flexible hours, no relevant experience required." Under normal circumstances, listings like this barely exist.

Scam listings favor the combination of high reward, low barrier, and instant hiring. When a job looks too good to be true, it usually is not true.

---

### Pattern 2: asking you to pay a "training fee" or "deposit" up front

A legitimate Taiwanese employer will never ask you to pay anything before you start work. It does not matter whether they call it a uniform fee, a system fee, a background check fee, or a training materials fee. If someone wants money from you before you have worked a day, treat it as a warning immediately.

This is one of the most typical job scam patterns there is.

---

### Pattern 3: the job is overseas, or they want you to leave the country for "training"

Cases like these have become very common in recent years, and foreign nationals make up most of the victims. The work location is usually somewhere in Southeast Asia, described as an "overseas branch" or "short-term training." In reality these are human trafficking or forced labor operations.

**If someone requires you to leave the country before you can start work, be extremely careful, and verify that the company genuinely exists in Taiwan before you travel.**

### Pattern 4: contact only through LINE or Instagram, with no formal company details

A normal Taiwanese company contacts you from a proper email address and can give you its full registered name, address, and unified business number.

If someone only messages you on social media, contacts you from a personal LINE account, cannot provide a company website or phone number, or if you find no trace of the company online at all, raise your guard.

---

### Pattern 5: requests for your personal documents or bank account

Before you start a job, a company may ask for identity documents for basic verification, and that is normal. But if someone asks for **your online banking password, or tells you to open a new account and hand control of it to them**, or wants to "hold on to" your original passport, these are extremely dangerous signals.

Scams like these are usually tied to money laundering and proxy accounts. Once you are involved, you may be breaking the law without knowing it.

---

## How to check whether a job is legitimate

### Look up the company registration

In Taiwan you can verify whether a company is legally registered through the **Ministry of Economic Affairs business registration search**. Enter the company name or unified business number and the basic details come up.

Website: [https://findbiz.nat.gov.tw](https://findbiz.nat.gov.tw)

### Check the employer's status

The Ministry of Labor's employment service systems let you look up approved legal employers, and check whether a company has a record of violations.

### Phone the company directly

If an opportunity leaves you uncertain, contact the company yourself through a public channel, meaning the number listed on its official website, and ask whether the opening is real. Scammers usually cannot keep the story going once you call.

---

## What to do about a suspicious listing

1. **Do not rush to accept, and do not rush to pay or submit documents.** Verify through several sources first
2. **Screenshot everything**, including the other party's account, the conversation, and any transfer requests
3. **Tell a friend or a senior you trust**, so someone else knows what is happening
4. **Report it**: call the Criminal Investigation Bureau's 165 anti-fraud hotline, or raise it with your school's career services office

**165 anti-fraud hotline (24 hours)**: dial 165

---

## In short

There are plenty of real, legitimate jobs in Taiwan waiting for international students, but staying alert through the search matters. Remember the basics: **do not pay, do not go abroad for training, confirm the company is legally registered, and never hand over your accounts or key documents.**

If an opportunity feels wrong, trust that instinct and check it out before you commit.
