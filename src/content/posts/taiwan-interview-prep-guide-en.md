Plenty of overseas Chinese and international students only discover once they are sitting in the room that interviews in Taiwan move at a different pace, and that the questions are framed differently from what they expected. Taiwanese companies follow a fairly fixed set of conventions. The questions vary little from one company to the next, but the way answers are scored and the etiquette around them differ in subtle ways. Knowing this in advance saves you a lot of avoidable mistakes.

---

## Common questions and how to answer them

### "Tell me about yourself."

Almost every interview opens here, and many candidates turn it into a recital of their resume. **Keep it to 2 or 3 minutes** and focus on the experience most relevant to this role, rather than starting from primary school.

A structure that works: who you are (background, education), what you have done (the 1 or 2 most relevant experiences), and why you are here (why this role and this company interest you).

If you are an international student, let your multilingual background and cross-cultural experience come through naturally. That is an advantage in itself, and there is no reason to talk around it.

---

### "What are your strengths and weaknesses?"

Taiwanese employers value honesty and self-awareness, and tend to distrust answers that sound too polished. Back a strength with a concrete example, and follow a weakness with what you are doing about it. Do not stop at "I'm too much of a perfectionist."

For instance: "I lose focus when I am juggling several things at once, so I started managing my day with a task list, and I get through work much more efficiently now." An answer like that earns more trust than pretending you have no weaknesses.

---

### "What are your salary expectations?"

Many candidates answer "anything is fine," which tends to read as either having no opinion or not knowing your own market value. **Do the market research beforehand**, give a reasonable range, then add that it is adjustable depending on the overall package and responsibilities.

Sources worth checking: published salary data for the same role, the salary ranges on comparable WPORT listings, or simply asking seniors from your school who already work in Taiwan.

---

### "Do you have any questions for us?"

**Never say no.** This question tests whether you are serious about the job.

Directions worth asking about:

- "How do new hires usually get up to speed in this role? What kind of work would the first three months look like?"
- "How is the team currently made up, how many people, and how is the work divided?"
- "What are the company's growth plans in this direction?"

Questions like these give you a clearer picture of the job, and they show the interviewer that you have actually been thinking.

## The unwritten rules of Taiwanese interviews

Answering the questions well is not quite enough. The Taiwanese workplace carries a few implicit rules that many foreign applicants never hear about.

### Be on time, which means be early

Punctuality is taken seriously in the Taiwanese workplace. **Aim to arrive 10 minutes early.** Do not walk in exactly on the hour, and certainly do not arrive late. If something goes wrong and you cannot make it, contact them before the interview rather than after.

Those extra minutes also let you take in the surroundings and settle yourself, instead of arriving out of breath.

### Address people by their title

In Taiwan, people are addressed by job title: 陳經理 (Manager Chen), 王主任 (Director Wang), 林協理 (Associate VP Lin). If you are not sure of someone's title, ask in the confirmation email when the interview is being scheduled.

Using someone's given name directly is generally read as too informal in a Taiwanese workplace.

### Send a thank-you note afterwards

A short thank-you email the same day or the next day is a small move that counts in your favor. It does not need to be long. "Thank you for your time today. The conversation gave me a much better understanding of XX, and I am even more interested in the opportunity" is enough.

This habit is less widespread in Taiwan than in some other countries, which is exactly why doing it makes you easy to remember.

---

## What international students should prepare for specifically

Beyond the general preparation above, Taiwanese employers will almost certainly ask two things: how good is your Chinese, and do you plan to stay in Taiwan long term?

**Be precise about your Chinese.** Do not gloss over it. If your spoken Chinese is fine but your writing is weaker, say so, and explain how you handle it. Honesty is far better than letting them discover the gap later.

**Take the stability question seriously too.** Employers worry about investing in training only to see you leave quickly. If you have plans to build a career in Taiwan, spell them out.

---

## In short

Taiwanese interview culture leans toward steadiness, honesty, and modesty, but that does not mean turning yourself into someone without a personality. Say what your cross-cultural background, language skills, and different perspective bring. Those are precisely the things you offer that local candidates do not.

Prepare early, rehearse a few times, and you will walk in more confident than you expect.
